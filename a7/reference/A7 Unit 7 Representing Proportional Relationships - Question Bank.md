## 7.01  Graphing Proportional Relationships

*MA.7.AR.4.3*

**Variation.**  Board 1 builds a table from a description and 2 votes on a point of the same story; 3 is an equation at three x's; 4 votes on a second point of a graphed line; 5 is a fraction whose x's must be chosen; 6 votes on four graphs with the cost axis counting by 2 — the pattern breaks here: two graphs pass through the same grid square, and only the scale tells them apart. 7 carries the room number as the extra number; 8 runs backwards; 9 is written. **Read this once, when you plan the unit.**

- The class last graphed lines from a table in Unit 11 (x = 0 and two more, the third point the check); today every table starts at the origin. The definition is said the same way every day: one quantity is always a constant multiple of the other, y = px. The number is the unit rate today; it is named the constant of proportionality tomorrow (7.02+03). 'Slope' and 'y-intercept' are on no student page. Read the scales first: Notes II's cost axis counts by 2, and board 6 turns on it.
- The Examples' question slides show an empty table beside an empty grid: fill and draw them on the projected slide. The boards read a table, a point or a graph; nobody draws a graph on a whiteboard. The key draws each graph on the student's own grid.
- Changed from the outline: none of its numbers. Its Notes III was dropped at the plan review (Barton 5); the deck has two Notes slides.

## 7.02  Constant of Proportionality: Table, Graph, Description

*MA.7.AR.4.2*

**Variation.**  Boards 1–2 read one table both ways (only which quantity is y changes); 3 votes on a bare table; 4 reads a graph scaled by 10, 5 votes on one scaled by 4, 6 votes on what a constant means; 7 is a description with an extra number (the snail's age). The pattern breaks at 8 — a negative constant from a table — and 9 judges a reversed ratio, written. The set: a table with a decimal, a table with a fraction constant, a graph by 1, a graph by 5 with its meaning, a description with the direction named, then a negative constant.

**Read this once, when you plan the unit.**

- Two book lessons (7.2 and 7.3) taught as one day. Built from the benchmark and the B1G-M first (the unit's Lesson Outlines, §7.02+03, standing decisions 2–6), with Math Nation read for scope only.
- Today names the constant of proportionality: yesterday's unit rate, the p in y = px, y ÷ x for every pair. Every constant is said with its unit (dollars per pound), and which quantity is y is said in every item: board 2 asks for the constant of board 1's table with the other quantity as y. Negative constants are taught in a bare context only (Example 2, board 8). No 'slope' on any page.

**What the audit against Math Nation found.**

- Every constant re-derived by sympy at build time as y ÷ x for every printed pair, decimals read as exact numbers; every graph drawn from y = px by u7/figs7.py, which refuses a marked point not on its line.
- Every wrong option is one named error: the ratio reversed, the scale ignored, a given number read off, a subtraction, or the constant read as a start.

**What changed from the book.**

- 7.2 and 7.3 (table; graph and description) → one day: Notes I–III, one Example each for a table and a graph, the description in Notes III and boards 6–7.
- Practice and Homework → the Independent Set: six questions, one feature changed at a time.

## 7.04  Is It Proportional?

*MA.7.AR.4.1*

**Variation.**  Boards 1–2 are tables, one feature changed (the y's): yes, then no. 3 votes on a table with only its last pair off; 4 is a straight line that misses the origin; 5 votes on points with no point at the origin, the y-axis scaled by 4. The pattern breaks at 6 — a select-all across tables, a graph and descriptions; 7 is the notebook story, its 50 pages the extra number; 8 is linear versus proportional on one grid; 9 is written. The independent set: a table that is, a constant difference, one pair off, a select-all of five, two lines, and an equation.

**Read this once, when you plan the unit.**

- The unit's fourth day. Built from the plan's section 7.04 (the unit's Lesson Outlines) and the benchmark's Source of Truth. The title slide names MA.7.AR.4.1; MA.8.AR.3.1 is taught the same day (Notes III, board 8, Independent Set 5–6, bank 9–10).
- The three tests in the unit's words, said the same way all period: y ÷ x is the same for every pair; a straight line through the origin; nothing added at the start. Ratios are tested with y ÷ x, never cross products (the state: they may lead to errors). Say slope here if you like; the slides do not.
- The state's named misconceptions are the wrong options: the origin not shown (board 5), every straight line proportional (boards 4, 8, 9), not testing every pair (board 3), rising taken for proportional.

**What the audit against Math Nation found.**

- Every ratio re-derived by sympy at build time as an exact y ÷ x, pair by pair; every table that is not proportional shown to have more than one ratio; every description tested at two values; every line checked at x = 0; every graph drawn from its equation by u7/figs7.py, which refuses a point marked 'on' that is on no line.

**What changed from the book.**

- Board 6: option E (parking, \$3 per hour) added — the kit sets five options on a select-all.

The pattern breaks at 6 — a table FROM an equation, its last x solved for; 7 is the plane story with its passengers as the extra number; 8 says an equation in a sentence; 9 is Lee's x ÷ y, written. The set breaks at 4: the same equation solved for the other variable.

**Read this once, when you plan the unit.**

- Two book lessons, 7.5 and 7.6, taught as one day: writing y = px from a table, a graph and a description, then going from any one form to the others. Built from the benchmark and its clarifications first (the unit's Lesson Outlines, §7.05+06, standing decisions 2, 4, 6 and 10).
- Florida writes p; say once, at the vocabulary slide, that some books write k. Notes III is the benchmark's own example: m = 60h and h = (1/60)m are one relationship, two constants — the question tells which variable to solve for, and the test asks it once (question 12b).

**Read this once, when you plan the unit.**
