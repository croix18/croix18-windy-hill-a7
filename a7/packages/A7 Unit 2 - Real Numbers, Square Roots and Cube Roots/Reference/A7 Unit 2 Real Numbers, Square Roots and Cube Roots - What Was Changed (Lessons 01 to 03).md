# Unit 2, Lessons 1–3 — what I found before building

Grade 7 Accelerated · 2.1 Square Values · 2.2 Square Values and Square Roots · 2.3 Cube Values

I worked every problem in the three teacher's editions, the three homework sheets and the three practice sheets, and checked every figure against its grid. Everything below is verified, with the page it appears on.

---

## 1. The one that has to be decided first

### Math Nation teaches that √16 = ±4. That is wrong, and it is wrong in a way this course cannot absorb.

The radical symbol denotes the **principal** — that is, the non-negative — square root. `√16 = 4`. The *equation* `x² = 16` has two solutions, `x = ±4`. Those are two different statements, and Lesson 2 collapses them.

Where it appears:

| Page | What it says |
|---|---|
| TE 134, Q2 | "Based on the information given about square roots, what is √16?" → keyed **4 and −4** |
| TE 134, Q3 | `√4 = ±2` · `√121 = ±11` |
| TE 136, Q2 | `√81 = ±9` · `√225 = ±15` · `√9 = ±3` |
| TE 137, Q1 | `√49 = ±7` · `√196 = ±14` |
| TE 135, Q1 | Xavier writes `√25 = 5`; the key marks him as having answered **one** of two problems correctly |
| Homework L2, Q5 | Printed on the student page: "The √36 = ±6. Explain why there are two solutions." |
| Homework L2, Q6–10 | `√81` `√−25` `√0` `√25` `√144` |
| Practice L2, Q5 | "The √16 = ±4. Explain why there are two solutions." |
| Practice L2, Q6–10 | `√100` `√−36` `√64` `√0` `√225` |

**Math Nation knows.** The teacher note on TE 136 says *"Students will be introduced to the principal square root in a future lesson."* So the definition on these pages is one they intend to retract in Lesson 4. And the note on TE 135 admits *"students would be correct if they selected the first two choices or the third"* for the value of √25 — then the answer key marks Xavier wrong for choosing one of them.

**Why it cannot be left alone.** Three other benchmarks in this same course require √ to have exactly one value:

- **MA.8.NSO.1.7** — order of operations *"including exponents and radicals."* If `√16` were `±4`, then `3 + √16` would be `7` or `−1`, and the benchmark would be unanswerable.
- **MA.8.NSO.1.2** — *"plot, order and compare square roots and cube roots."* You cannot plot a two-valued object on a number line.
- **MA.8.GR.1.1** — the Pythagorean Theorem. A hypotenuse of `±5` is not a length.

**What MA.8.AR.2.3 actually says.** Its clarification is *"focus on understanding that solving x² = p gives both a positive and a negative solution."* That is a statement about the **equation**. Florida never attaches the ± to the symbol. Math Nation moved it, and the move breaks three benchmarks to simplify one.

The correct version is not harder to teach — it is one extra line:

> `x² = 25` has two solutions, because both `5 × 5` and `(−5) × (−5)` give 25. We write `x = ±√25 = ±5`.
> `√25` on its own means the positive one: `√25 = 5`. That is why we have to write the `±` ourselves.

That distinction *is* the lesson. Taught this way, Lesson 2 gets a genuine difficulty instead of a false rule.

## 2. Other mathematical errors on printed pages

**"The graph of y = x² only has positive y-values."** False at the origin: `0² = 0`, which is not positive. Appears three times, twice on student pages:

- TE 124, 2.1.4 Q1d — the completed sentence reads *"for any value of x multiplied by itself, the value of y will always be positive."* The key forces **always** over **sometimes**, which makes the printed sentence false, then patches it with a trailing clause: *"so all of the points other than (0,0) are above the x-axis."* The sentence contradicts its own ending.
- **Homework L3 Q4** and **Practice L3 Q4** — *"The graph of y = x² only has positive y-values"* is given to students as a fact they must then explain.
- **Homework L1 Q10** — *"Why is the graph of y = x² above the x-axis?"* presupposes it. (The Practice sheet asks the same thing correctly: *"Will the graph of y = x² ever go below the x-axis? Explain why or why not."* The homework should simply borrow that wording.)

Fix: **non-negative**, or "never goes below the x-axis." One word.

**TE 121, 2.1.3 Q3b — the answer teaches the misconception MA.8.NSO.1.1 tests against.** The question asks whether a square can have an area of 8. The printed answer: *"It is possible if the measure of the sides of the square are decimals or fractions."* The side is `√8 ≈ 2.828…`, which is **irrational** — not a decimal, not a fraction. Math Nation's own teacher note says *"The intent is not to introduce side lengths of √8,"* which is fine — but the printed student answer then asserts something false, in a unit whose next lesson lists MA.8.NSO.1.1 (rational versus irrational) as a benchmark. The answer needs only to say "somewhere between 2 and 3, and not a whole number" and stop.

**TE 133 — "Explain that the index can be any rational number."** The index of a radical is a positive integer of at least 2. This sentence is teacher-facing and would mislead anyone who acted on it.

**TE 131 — the generalization and the next question disagree.** The boxed rule reads *"For equations in the form x² = p, where p is a **whole number**…"* and question 4 immediately uses `x² = −36`. Whole numbers are not negative. The rule needs a different word, or the question needs a different frame.

## 3. Figures and grids

**TE 146 — the answer key plots a point off its own grid.** In 2.3.4 the table produces `(−2, −8)`, but the printed grid's y-axis stops at **−7**. The key's red dot for that point sits in the white space below the last gridline, with nothing under it. Meanwhile the x-axis runs from −11 to 11 for a table whose x-values never leave −2 to 2. The grid needs to be −3 to 3 by −9 to 9.

**Homework L1 Q9 and Practice L1 Q9 — three of five points cannot be plotted.** Both grids run 0 to 5 vertically with minor lines every 0.2, and both tables ask for points that land between them:

| Sheet | x | y | Nearest gridline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Homework | −1.4 | **1.96** | 2.0 — a student cannot show the difference |
| Homework | 2/5 | **0.16** | 0.2 — same |
| Practice | −1 3/5 | **2.56** | 2.6 |
| Practice | −0.4 | **0.16** | 0.2 |
| Practice | 1.2 | **1.44** | 1.4 |

The arithmetic is good practice. The instruction "then plot the point on the graph" is not honest on this grid. Either the x-values move to halves and whole numbers, or the grid gets finer, or the plotting instruction comes off.

**TE 120 — checked, and it is correct.** The square labelled 8 by 8 with area 64 measures exactly 8 grid cells on each side. I measured it because the aspect looked off at low resolution; it is fine.

## 4. Benchmark boundaries

MA.8.AR.2.3 states its range explicitly: **perfect squares up to 225**, and **perfect cubes from −125 to 125**. Every square in these three lessons is inside the range. The cubes are not:

| Item | Value | Inside −125 to 125? |
|---|---|---|
| Homework L3 Q3 | cube of volume 216 | no |
| Homework L3 Q6 | `∛343` | no |
| Homework L3 Q10 | `n³ = 512` | no |
| Practice L3 Q6 | `∛216` | no |
| Practice L3 Q8 | `x³ = 1,728` | no — 12³ |
| Practice L3 Q10 | `m³ = −343` | no |
| TE 148, Q4 table | `w³ = 216` | no |

Six of the twenty cube items on the two student sheets, plus one in the guided table, sit outside the range the test will use. Nothing here is *wrong* — an accelerated class can certainly cube 12 — but it is worth deciding on purpose rather than by inheritance. See the questions at the end.

**Separately, this is where the test is weakest.** On the 2025 released items, *"selecting all solutions to x³ = 64"* scored **26% correct** — one of the six lowest scores on the entire Grade 8 FAST, and it is precisely this unit's benchmark. Two things follow. The item was a **select-all**, a format that appears nowhere in these three lessons. And the trap is almost certainly students selecting both `4` and `−4` for a cube — which is the exact confusion the ± error in section 1 will make worse.

The B1G-M lists the matching misconception: *"believing that because a negative number has no real square root, it has no real cube root either."* Lesson 3 never addresses it head on, and it is the best candidate for that lesson's one hard slide.

Two of these need real rebuilding rather than a wording change. The 2.1.3 initials table is a circulate-and-collect-signatures activity, and both Explorations in 2.1 are written as student-directed partner discovery with the teacher deliberately staying out — *"this should be a primarily student-directed Exploration."* Turning that into individual work is not a find-and-replace; it becomes taught notes plus independent practice.

**Two of the three warm-ups have no mathematics in them, and the third has none connected to the lesson.**

- **2.1.1** — a growth-mindset reflection. Two questions, both "answers will vary," no numbers. This is the same category as the career-profile video I replaced in Unit 1.
- **2.2.1** — the deep-sea clam. The arithmetic checks out (8.4 mm per 100 years × 5 ≈ 42 mm ≈ 1⅔ inches, and the two units agree), but it is an estimation problem with no bearing on square roots.
- **2.3.1** — a 3×3 magic square. A fine puzzle, unrelated to cubes. The key says "answers will vary"; there are exactly 8 arrangements, all rotations and reflections of one.

- **2.3.6** — this one is good and I would keep its mathematics: `∛125`, `∛−8`, `w³ = −64`, `1 = k³`. Note it instructs students **not** to use a calculator, which cuts against your standing policy; I would drop that line and keep the questions.

## 6. What is worth keeping

Not everything here needs work, and some of it is better than Unit 1's material.

- **The concrete-to-abstract spine of 2.1 is genuinely good.** Draw squares on a grid → `A = s²` → `y = x²` → graph the pairs → notice y is never negative. That progression earns its 15 minutes and I would not flatten it.
- **TE 121, question 3** — *"can a square have an area of 8?"* — is the best question in the three lessons. It is the seed of irrational numbers, and it is asked at exactly the right moment. Only the printed answer needs repair.
- **TE 134's parenthesis note** — `−4² = −16` because it means `−(4 × 4)`, while `(−4)² = 16` — is a real distinction, correctly explained, and it is a FAST trap.
- **2.3.3's similarities-and-differences table** between squares and cubes is worth keeping as written notes; only the "discuss with your partner" framing has to go.
- **The 2.3.5 cube-root table** (equation → related cube root → solution) is a clean piece of notation practice. It only needs its 216 row retuned.
