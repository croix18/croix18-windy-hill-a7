# What Was Changed — Unit 2 Assessment

Math Nation's `7a.02_Assessment` and `7a.02_Assessment_Guide`, worked question by question before anything was rebuilt. Every keyed answer on their test was verified numerically. **All of their answers were arithmetically correct.** What was wrong was one false statement, the scoring, the precision two items demanded, and what the test never asked about.

The full defect list is a separate document. This is the short version of what the rebuild did about each finding.

---

## The one mathematical error

**Their Question 5 was false as written.**

> "equations in the form x² = p, where p is a **whole number**, will have [two solutions]"

Whole numbers include 0, and x² = 0 has exactly one solution. Their own Assessment Guide states the correct condition on the facing page — "will always have two solutions **when the value of p is greater than 0**" — but the item on the test dropped it. Their scoring rule then said "no partial credit," so a student who selected **only one solution** — the right answer for p = 0 — scored 0 out of 6 with a printed note suggesting they had guessed.

**Now:** the item reads "where p is a whole number **greater than 0**," and a new part c asks directly how many solutions x² = 0 has. The defect became the teaching point.

---

## The count

Their guide claimed 100 points and said teachers could "determine how to use the extra 2 points." The six questions sum to **98**. There were no extra points; the guide was 2 short.

**Now:** nine questions summing to exactly 100, with the point value printed in the right margin of every question and a `Score _____ / 100` line in the student header.

## The two items decided in the second decimal place

Their Question 1 asked for the point closest to each value on a number line ruled in halves.

- **√3 = 1.7321.** The midpoint between the two candidate points was 1.75. **The margin was 0.018.** Worse, the estimation method Lesson 4 teaches — 3 is nearer 4 than 1, so √3 is nearer 2 — produces ≈ 1.8, which selects the wrong letter. The item punished the taught procedure.
- **2π − 9 = −2.7168.** Choosing correctly required π > 3.125, a margin of 0.033. π ≈ 3.14 works; π ≈ 3.1 does not; π ≈ 3 lands exactly on the wrong letter.

**Now:** the same three-part item, retuned.

| | Value | Margin from the midpoint | Survives |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | 2 − π ≈ −1.14 | 0.108 | any π from 2.75 to 3.25 |
| b | √6 ≈ 2.45 | 0.199 | any estimate above 2.25 |
| c | ∛9 − 8 ≈ −5.92 | 0.170 | any estimate of ∛9 below 2.25 |

Their part c was well built and was kept as it was. The number line itself was redrawn with **coordinates above the line and point letters below** — on their version, −4, −1, 0 and 2.5 sat in the same row, same size and same spacing as the point names, which is why their guide had to pre-write a rule deducting 3 points per part for the misreading it expected.

## What was not tested, and now is

**The principal square root.** The centerpiece of this unit — √64 = 8 while x² = 64 has two solutions — appeared nowhere on their test. Their Question 3 tested the equation side only, so a student who still believed √64 = ±8 could score 98 out of 98. **New Question 3** asks for √121, then the solutions of x² = 121, then ∛−125, then the solutions of x³ = −125, side by side.

**Plotting.** MA.8.NSO.1.2 reads "plot, order and compare." Their test asked students to read letters off a finished number line and never to draw a point. **New Question 8** gives a blank line and four values.

**x³ = a positive perfect cube.** Their select-all had x³ = −64 but no x³ = 64. The 2025 released FAST item "select all solutions to x³ = 64" scored **26% statewide, among the six lowest on the entire exam, tied for fifth.** **New Question 4** has four rows: x² = 64, x³ = 64, x³ = −64, x² = −64.

**Estimating between two integers.** The core MA.8.NSO.1.1 skill and the whole of Lesson 4, tested on their version only inside the number-line item. **New Question 2** asks it directly for √54, ∛100, −√29 and 2π.

## Smaller corrections

**Two of their four comparisons contained no irrational number.** 9/4 vs (3/4)·∛8 is 2.25 vs 1.5; (4)² vs √256 is 16 vs 16. Both were tagged MA.8.NSO.1.2 — *plot, order and compare rational **and irrational** numbers* — so half of that question's points did not test the benchmark named on it. The rebuilt comparison question has five parts and four of them involve an irrational value; the fifth is the equality, kept so that "=" is a live option rather than a decoy.

**"not a real solution" is now "no real solutions."** The equation does not have one solution that fails to be real. It has none.

**The rational / irrational sort is now 3–3.** Theirs ran 4 irrational to 2 rational, so a student who wrote everything in the irrational column collected 24 of 36 points for making no decision. The new list also includes √81 ÷ 3, which looks irrational and equals 3 — the discriminator their list was missing.

**√12 / 2 was replaced.** It equals √3, which was already Question 1b on their test. Twenty printed values, nineteen distinct numbers.

---

## Calculators

Their header printed **"No Calculator"** on the student page, and their guide states all questions are no-calculator.

The substance is right — with a calculator, questions 1, 2, 6, 7 and 9 become button-pushing and measure nothing. The printing is the problem. **Nothing about calculator policy appears anywhere on the student pages.** The teacher's note carries it; you announce it.

---

## Length

Six questions became nine; three pages became four. Two of their six carried 55% of the grade, so a single misread of a number-line scale moved a student 9 points and a rounding of π moved them 6. No question on the rebuilt test carries more than 16.
